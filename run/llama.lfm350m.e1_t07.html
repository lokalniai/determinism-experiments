The sky appears blue during the day due to a phenomenon called Rayleigh scattering. This occurs when sunlight interacts with particles in the Earth's atmosphere, primarily nitrogen and oxygen molecules. The shorter wavelengths of light—such as blue—are scattered more effectively than longer wavelengths like red or orange. This scattering process, known as atmospheric diffusion, is more pronounced at shorter wavelengths, which are scattered out of our line of sight. Sunsets, on the other hand, are often red or orange because of a phenomenon called Doppler shift and atmospheric scattering. During a sunset, the Earth's surface heats up and emits light, while the Sun is much closer and emits a slightly redder light. The Sun's light passes through the atmosphere, where sunlight is scattered by molecules and aerosols. The longer wavelengths dominate this scattered light, giving it a reddish hue. This effect is particularly noticeable in areas with high levels of pollution, as dust or aerosols in the atmosphere can scatter the light in ways that enhance red or orange colors. Additionally, the presence of water vapor and other gases can further modify the color of the sunset. Atmospheric conditions such as pollution, dust, and water vapor can significantly alter the appearance of the sky and the colors of sunset. For instance, pollution can introduce additional colored particles into the atmosphere, which scatter light in complex ways that can make the sky appear more muted or red. Dust particles can act as tiny mirrors, bouncing light back and forth and further contributing to the reddish appearance. These factors combine to create the dynamic and often visually striking colors we observe during sunsets.
